The prohibition

A nonzero object cannot be more divisible than its size allows. Here is the form this takes in the proof, and what it needs in order to work.

Stepanov's second move

In Stepanov's proof the prohibition is one line: a polynomial that is not identically zero has no more zeros, counted with multiplicity, than its degree. Many solutions, each a zero of high order, force the degree up, and that bounds the count.

For whole numbers the same idea reads: a nonzero whole number divisible by many primes, many times, cannot be small. The proof needs this not for one number but for maps between lattices, the many-dimensional cousins of whole numbers.

Slopes

A lattice with a way to measure lengths has an arithmetic slope: roughly, the average logarithm of how densely it is packed, per dimension. A lattice that is divisible by a prime is packed more densely, so its slope goes up.

Bost's slope inequality says that a nonzero map from one such lattice to another cannot raise the slope by more than the size of the map. In plain words: an injective map of lattices cannot be too divisible compared with its size. This is the exact analogue of "no more zeros than the degree", and in the proof it plays that part.

Why one map is not enough

One map controls only the largest slope of the target lattice, its single most divisible direction. The proof needs control in every direction at once, so it uses many maps whose images spread evenly over the target.

As we read the archive, this is done with a nearly orthogonal frame: a family of subspaces, together about 20 times the dimension of the target, that overlap no more than random ones would. Such a frame comes from the discrete series of a large unitary group U(r), through Schur orthogonality and the fast decay of matrix coefficients.

The constant here looks frightening: a rank r of at least 1801. It is a safety margin, not a sharp threshold; we checked by computer that it is not where the argument is tight. The large gap between parameters that the archive also asks for does its work elsewhere, in the counting theorem behind the next support.

Two sides of one identity

Rubinstein's guide describes the step that spreads the images evenly through a relative trace formula of Jacquet and an approximate orthogonality. We had described it through the discrete series of U(r) and Schur orthogonality. Reading the proof of LinkS in the code shows that these are two sides of the same argument, not two different arguments. All the declarations below are on the list of what the final theorem depends on (ishaan2006/rh-lean-reproduction, results/used_decls.txt.gz).

The central statement, theorem S (ThmSOwn), is by definition four bounds on the cross kernel Kmat between two words u and u′ (thmSOwn_of_clauses): two on the diagonal and two off it. On the diagonal the kernel is the identity up to a small error; off the diagonal it is small in the Hilbert–Schmidt norm. That is the approximate orthogonality.

Both readings agree on what the step must deliver: images spread evenly enough that the slope inequality controls every direction.

How it fits

The ladder makes the objects very divisible: m times the local gain. The size is known exactly. The prohibition says divisibility cannot exceed size. Set the two against each other, and what is left is a bound on the error in the prime count, with the length of the ladder still free. Choosing it well is the balance.

The same plain fact sits underneath all of this: a nonzero whole number is at least 1. It appears in the count of divisibility, in the slope inequality, and in the integer that closes the hardest case. We offer this as an observation, not as a claim about the proof.
