Stepanov and the new proof, side by side

The proof follows Stepanov's three moves over the whole numbers. Here is the match, row by row, and the four places where it stops being a match. Those four places are where, as we read it, the new ideas are.

Read down the left column and you have Stepanov's proof; read down the right and you have the skeleton of the new one. The two are the same shape. The author himself calls the first step of his argument a "Stepanov determinant".

Where the analogy breaks

1. There is no single Frobenius

Stepanov's whole method rests on one fact that holds at every point at once: xp = x. The whole numbers have nothing like it. Each prime has its own clock arithmetic and its own Frobenius, and they do not fit together.

The new proof does not look for a global replacement. It splits the work of the one tool into three roles: a local Frobenius at every inert prime, the size of the objects, and the sorting of the primes. All three come from one field with complex multiplication, and that is why they agree. In Stepanov's proof the agreement is free, because there is only one tool. Here it has to be built, and building it is, as we read it, the heart of the architecture.

2. Cheap steps are not free any more

In Stepanov's degree count, each derivative raises the degree of the coefficients by at most n − 1. That bound comes for free, and it is what keeps the second term of his balance at (n − 1)m. The shape of the balance follows from it.

In the new proof the corresponding fact is that each rung of the ladder must take whole-number objects to whole-number objects at a small, fixed price. Nothing makes that automatic. If the price grew with the length of the ladder, the balance would be x/m + m², the best length would be x1/3, and the argument would stop at 2/3 instead of 1/2. Keeping the price down takes some of the deepest mathematics in the archive. This row has no counterpart on Stepanov's side: it is where the comparison stops helping.

3. "Not zero" becomes a statement about a family

Stepanov shows his polynomial is not zero in one line: two kinds of terms have degrees of different parity and cannot cancel. That is where his hypothesis "n odd" comes from.

The new proof cannot work with one object. It needs a whole family, and then it needs almost every member to be nonzero where it counts: degenerate local types must be rare, about 1/q, and the family must follow those weights. On top of that, the hardest case of the price per rung is closed by a single whole number, and everything hinges on that number not being zero. The same plain fact, a nonzero whole number is at least 1, is used three times.

4. From some primes to all primes, and from a bound to zeros

Stepanov counts points for each p separately, and the bound is the result. The new proof first controls only the inert primes of one field, then has to recover all primes, and at the end turns a bound on long windows into the absence of zeros with a lemma of Landau's type. Neither step has a counterpart in 1969. As we read them, they are known in kind and carry no new idea, but they make the right-hand column longer than the left.

Another way to see the same thing

Rubinstein's guide frames the proof through Chebyshev rather than Stepanov: Chebyshev bounded prime sums by the divisibility of factorials, and the new proof is that argument run with a far better integer. To us the two frames are the same idea seen from two sides. Chebyshev explains the first column of the dictionary, what is being counted; Stepanov explains why the exponent is a balance. The author's phrase "Stepanov determinant" points our way, but both readings agree on how the proof is built.

The author's own view goes a step further (post of 24 November 2026). He treats arguments like this one as raw material for finding the right definitions. Over finite fields, the proofs of Weil and Deligne rest on an object of the kind written H¹ (for a curve it can be built from its Jacobian), and the Frobenius acts on it; over number fields no such object is known. He expects the Riemann Hypothesis to be easy once the right setup exists, and reads any real difficulty as a sign that the present setup is clumsy. In the terms of this page: the first row of the dictionary, the missing single Frobenius, is where such an object would act, and the long right-hand column is the price of not having it. That last sentence is our gloss, not his.

A historical echo, offered only as that: when Hasse proved the bound for elliptic curves in the 1930s, he used complex multiplication. Complex multiplication is at the centre of the new proof too. We do not claim any link beyond the echo.
