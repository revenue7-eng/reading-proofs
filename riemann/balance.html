The balance

Why the bound in the proof comes out as x to the power one half, and why a slightly more expensive step would have lost it.

The architecture of a proof   The map

Two terms pulling against each other

In our reading, the bound at the end of the proof is a trade-off between two terms. One is x/m: it falls as the chain of lifts gets longer. The other is the price of a chain of length m. A longer chain makes the first term smaller and the second larger, so there is a best length, and the size of the bound is decided there.

If the price grows like m, the best length is √x and the bound is about 2√x, which is x1/2: the exponent of the Riemann Hypothesis. If the price grew like m², the best length would be near x1/3 and the bound would be of size x2/3. That is a true statement too, but it is not the Riemann Hypothesis.

What this does and does not show

This is arithmetic, not the proof. It shows only why the cost of one step decides the exponent. That the cost in the proof really grows like m is the hard part, and it is where the chain of theta lifts does its work. The reading is ours.
