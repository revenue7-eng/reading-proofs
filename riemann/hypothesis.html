The Riemann Hypothesis

What the statement says, before any proof: one function, the places where it is zero, and what those places tell us about the prime numbers.

A function built from the primes

Add up the reciprocals of all whole numbers raised to a power s:

ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + …

The sum makes sense when s is larger than 1. Euler noticed in the eighteenth century that, because every whole number splits into primes in exactly one way, the same sum is a product with one factor for each prime:

ζ(s) = ∏p 1 / (1 − p−s)

So ζ is a way of packing all the primes into one function. In 1859 Riemann let s be a complex number, s = σ + it, and showed that the function extends to the whole complex plane, with a single exception at s = 1, where it becomes infinite. He also found a symmetry: the values at s and at 1 − s are tied by an explicit formula, the functional equation.

Where the function is zero

Some zeros are easy to find. The functional equation forces ζ to vanish at −2, −4, −6, and so on. These are called the trivial zeros. Every other zero lies in the strip where the real part σ is strictly between 0 and 1, and the zeros there come in mirror pairs across the vertical line σ = 1/2.

The Riemann Hypothesis. Every zero of ζ other than the trivial ones has real part exactly 1/2.

What was known before November 2026:

  • No zero has real part 1. Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin proved this in 1896, and it is exactly what gives the prime number theorem: the number of primes up to x is about x / ln x.
  • Infinitely many zeros lie on the line (Hardy, 1914), and more than 40 per cent of them do (Conrey, 1989).
  • Every zero with imaginary part between 0 and 3·1012 is on the line, checked rigorously by computer (Platt and Trudgian, 2021).

None of this excludes a zero off the line further up. That is the whole question.

Why the zeros matter for the primes

Let π(x) be the number of primes up to x. The best simple approximation to it is the logarithmic integral li(x), the area under 1/ln t from 0 to x. Riemann's explicit formula writes the difference π(x) − li(x) as a sum of waves, one for each zero. A zero with real part σ produces a wave whose size grows like xσ. If every zero sits on the line, every wave grows like √x, and the primes are as evenly spread as they can be. A single zero further to the right would make the error larger, at that rate, forever.

This is an exact equivalence. Von Koch showed in 1901 that the hypothesis holds if and only if π(x) − li(x) is at most a constant times √x · ln x. In 1976 Schoenfeld made the constant explicit: the hypothesis implies

|π(x) − li(x)| < √x · ln x / (8π)    for every x ≥ 2657

So the hypothesis is, in the end, a statement about counting primes. The exponent 1/2 is the size of the error.

It can even be said with no zeta function and no primes in sight. Let σ(n) be the sum of the divisors of n. Robin proved in 1984 that the hypothesis is equivalent to

σ(n) < eγ · n · ln ln n    for every n > 5040

where γ = 0.5772… is Euler's constant. At n = 5040 itself the inequality fails: σ(5040) = 19 344, while the right side is about 19 237. Lagarias gave a similar version in 2002, with harmonic numbers in place of ln ln n. Both are now theorems in Lean: Sutherland combined the archive with an independent formalization of Robin's and Lagarias's criteria (kimihiro64/Robin1984) and put the two inequalities, stated with no hypotheses, through the same kernel checks.

What this check cannot do: test the hypothesis. Every x in this range, and far beyond it, is already settled without the hypothesis: the inequality is proved unconditionally for 2657 ≤ x ≤ 1.1·1026 from the computed zeros (Johnston, 2021). The table shows the size of error the hypothesis promises, not evidence for it.

The generalization: Dirichlet L-functions

To count primes in a progression, say primes that leave remainder 3 when divided by 10, one weights each 1/ns by a Dirichlet character χ, a function that repeats with period q and respects multiplication. The result is a Dirichlet L-function L(s, χ), with its own Euler product over the primes and its own functional equation. The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions says the same thing for each of them: apart from trivial zeros, every zero has real part 1/2. It controls how evenly primes are spread among the remainders, with error of size √x, just as the original controls π(x). The zeta function is the case of the trivial character.

The exact statement in the archive

The archive does not define the hypothesis itself. Its main theorem uses the definition in Mathlib, Lean's mathematics library, written before the announcement:

def RiemannHypothesis : Prop :=
  ∀ (s : ℂ) (_ : riemannZeta s = 0)
    (_ : ¬∃ n : ℕ, s = -2 * (n + 1)) (_ : s ≠ 1),
    s.re = 1 / 2

theorem riemannHypothesis : RiemannHypothesis

Read aloud: for every complex s, if ζ(s) = 0, s is not one of −2, −4, −6, …, and s is not 1, then the real part of s is 1/2. The same Mathlib file proves that riemannZeta is the usual function: it equals the sum of 1/ns when the real part of s is above 1, it has the trivial zeros, and it satisfies the functional equation. At s = 1 Mathlib gives the function an arbitrary value, so excluding that point weakens nothing.

Main.lean proves five theorems. Besides this one, a second states the same thing in the archive's own words, and Lean accepts the two as equal by definition. The other three concern Dirichlet L-functions: no zeros with real part above 1/2 for every character, primitive or not; zeros in the strip lie on the line, for every character; and the same for primitive characters, the form in which the generalized hypothesis is usually stated. The independent checks found that all five match the statements in the challenge file, which imports only Mathlib.

Half a strip is enough

The map ends on a weaker-looking line: no Dirichlet L-function has a zero with real part above 1/2. In the archive all five theorems follow from that one statement, which the code calls EveryCharacter. The reason is the mirror symmetry. The functional equation pairs a zero at s with a zero at 1 − s (for ζ), or with a zero of the conjugate character's L-function at the mirror point (for a primitive character). A zero to the left of the line would therefore have a partner to the right. Rule out the right half of the strip for every character at once, and the left half is ruled out too; what remains is the line. This step is classical, and in the archive it uses only Mathlib.

So the whole proof is aimed at one thing: showing that nothing on the right of the line can be a zero. How it does that is the rest of this site; from primes to zeros follows the chain from the prime sum to this statement. The top row of the map is the chain of conclusions that ends here, why only Dirichlet explains where the method stops, and Stepanov's method is the 1969 proof of the same kind of statement for curves over finite fields that our reading starts from.
