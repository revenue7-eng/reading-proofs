The architecture of a proof

A proof of the Riemann Hypothesis has been announced, written only for a machine. We read it to understand how it is built. This is what we found.

What this is

On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge announced, in a four-page note, that he and Claude had proved the Riemann Hypothesis and its generalization to all Dirichlet L-functions. The proof exists only as an archive of Lean code: about 175,000 files and 1.5 million declarations by our count. There is no human-readable version of the argument.

We wrote up what we understood as an article: How we understood the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.

We read the archive's source, layer by layer, to understand the proof. This site is our reading. It is not the author's account, and where our understanding may be wrong, we say so.

The author has since said that the archive is full of unnecessary material and that most of it will probably turn out not to be needed (post of 24 November 2026). That is why we checked every name we followed against the list of declarations the final theorem actually uses. Every Lean name on the map is one the final theorem depends on; cards without a Lean name, such as Poitou–Tate or Ribet's congruences, are our identification by type.

Status

The statement the archive proves is the formal definition of the Riemann Hypothesis in Mathlib, Lean's mathematics library. The proof has not been peer reviewed, and the author has not published a version for people to read. We do not claim the hypothesis is proved in the classical sense. We describe how the formal argument is built.

The idea in one paragraph

In 1969 Sergei Stepanov found an elementary proof of the analogue of the Riemann Hypothesis for curves over finite fields. His argument relies on one tool, the Frobenius map, which the ordinary integers do not have. As we read it, the new proof carries Stepanov's method over to the integers by splitting the work of that one tool into three roles: a single field with complex multiplication supplies a substitute at every relevant prime, a chain of theta lifts supplies multiplicity, and characters of an abelian field sort the primes. The exponent 1/2 then appears as the balance point between two errors, together with a bound, obtained on average, for the twisted prime sums of the auxiliary characters.

How to read the map

  • Top: the chain of conclusions, from a count of divisibility over the primes to the absence of zeros with real part above 1/2.
  • Columns: steps 2 to 4 of the argument, and the four supports the construction rests on.
  • White cards: known results from the literature.
  • Pink cards: what we read as new in this proof. The proof is by Alpöge and Claude; we do not know which of them each idea came from.
  • Third line of a card: its name in the Lean archive and how deep we read it: down to the published source, or only its statement.

The parts of the map

Each part of the map has its own page, with a live check where a simple and honest one exists.

  • The frame: Stepanov's method, the 1969 proof the whole reading rests on, and the two proofs side by side.
  • Steps: a Frobenius at every inert prime, the right size, the ladder of lifts, the prohibition, the balance.
  • Supports: whole numbers on every rung, one integer for every l, few bad members, the family exists.
