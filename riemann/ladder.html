The ladder of lifts

How the proof collects one small gain many times over, and the piece of algebra that lets it.

The problem it solves

At every inert prime p, the field at the centre of the proof gives the objects a small gain in divisibility: 1/p + 1/p³ + 1/p⁵ + … each time. One such gain is far too small to beat the error in the prime count.

Stepanov met the same problem over finite fields and solved it with multiplicity. His polynomial vanished not once but l times at every point, and l was allowed to grow with the problem. The proof needs a way to make its gain grow in the same way. Its answer is a ladder.

How one rung works

A theta lift is a classical way to carry an object from one symmetry group to a larger one. The proof builds a chain of m such lifts. As we read it, three things happen on every rung.

  1. The rung adds a block. The object at each level is described, at each prime p, by a list of numbers called Satake parameters. A lift keeps the old list and appends a new block to it: a run of powers of p, symmetric about the middle. This is the Howe–Rallis picture of the lift.
  2. A polynomial erases the block. There is a polynomial in Hecke operators, with whole-number coefficients, that wipes out everything the new blocks contribute. On the lifted object it acts as a power of p times the operator of the very first level, however many rungs there are.
  3. So the gain comes back. Because the added blocks are erased, the local gain from the first level is collected again on this rung. After m rungs it has been collected m times, and the gains add up.

The division of labour is clean. How much one rung gains is decided by the field. That the gain repeats m times is the work of the Hecke polynomial.

The algebra behind it, to try

Step 2 has a shadow in plain algebra, an identity about symmetric polynomials. Write B for the original parameters and A for everything the rungs added. Let ek be the sum of all products of k different numbers from a list, and hk the same with repeats allowed. Then for every r:

Σl=0…r (−1)l hl(A) · er−l(A ∪ B) = er(B)

The left side is built from the lifted list A ∪ B, yet it comes out equal to something that depends on B alone: the added blocks cancel completely. Choose the numbers below and see.

Here each block is the run 1, p, …, pn−1, a rescaled copy of the symmetric runs in the proof; the identity holds for any numbers at all. The arithmetic is exact, with whole numbers of any size. We checked the same identity by computer while reading the archive.

The rungs themselves are classical. What we take to be new is the use of the ladder: a chain whose length grows with the problem, so that a fixed local gain becomes as large as needed.

Why the length has to grow

By a theorem of Littlewood, the error in the prime count really does swing on the scale of √x. A construction tuned once and for all cannot hit that scale exactly, so something has to grow with x. Here it is the ladder, and the best length is m ≈ √x. Why √x, and why it would be x1/3 if each rung cost more, is shown on the balance page.

Keeping each rung cheap is its own story: every rung must take whole-number objects to whole-number objects, prime by prime, at a small and known price. The hardest case of that accounting is closed by one whole number.

What we have and have not read

This page is our reading of the formal proof, not the author's account. We followed the pieces above down to their published sources, except the first, which we read as a statement.
