A Frobenius at every inert prime

The local structure that stands in for Stepanov's missing tool, and where the small gain at each prime comes from.

The missing tool

Stepanov's proof over a finite field with q elements rests on one map, the Frobenius: raise every number to the power q. It fixes exactly the solutions over that field, and it respects addition. With it he builds a polynomial that vanishes many times at every solution.

The ordinary whole numbers have no such map. Each prime p has its own clock arithmetic with its own Frobenius, and they do not fit together. The proof does not look for one global map. It finds a local stand-in at every prime of the right kind, all cut from the same object.

Where the stand-in comes from

The central object of the proof is a field with complex multiplication. Its simplest model is an elliptic curve with the same symmetry, and we describe the mechanism on that model, as the archive's statements allow.

Take an imaginary quadratic field K. A prime p is inert if it stays prime in K; about half of all primes are. At every inert prime the curve has supersingular reduction (Deuring, 1941). That means its formal group, the law of adding points near zero written as a power series, has height 2.

In the archive the logarithm of this group is the series

log(x) = Σj ≥ 0 xp2j / pj

Its coefficients are fractions. Rescale the coordinate by ϖ, a number with ϖp²−1 = p. Then the coefficients become whole numbers, and modulo p the series is just x + xp². The second term is the Frobenius of the field with p² elements. There is the stand-in, at every inert prime at once.

Check it

After rescaling, the coefficient of xp2j is p raised to the power (p2j − 1)/(p² − 1) − j. The table computes these exponents exactly. An exponent 0 means the term survives modulo p; anything larger means it vanishes.

Dwork's radius

To turn the congruence into divisibility, the proof puts the part that is divisible by p under an exponential, with a normalising factor 1/(p − 1). The p-adic exponential Σ yⁿ/n! has whole-number coefficients exactly when y is divisible to order at least 1/(p − 1): Dwork's radius. The proof is one line of Legendre's formula: p divides n! exactly (n − s(n))/(p − 1) times, where s(n) is the sum of the digits of n in base p.

Below, choose how divisible y is. The tool finds the worst term yⁿ/n! up to n = p⁴. At the radius every term is whole; just below it, the terms at n = p, p², … are not.

We checked both facts by computer at p = 5 while reading: the congruence holds exactly modulo p, and the threshold is sharp.

The gain

Put together, at every inert prime p the objects of the proof gain divisibility with weight

p / (p² − 1) = 1/p + 1/p³ + 1/p⁵ + …

only odd powers of p. That is exactly the weight 1 − χK(pⁱ) of the first step, which is 2 on odd powers and 0 on even ones. Both the odd powers and the height 2 come from one fact: the residue field at an inert prime has p² elements. At p = 5 the weight is 5/24.

One gain per prime is small. Collecting it many times is the work of the ladder of lifts.

What we have and have not read

This page is our reading of the formal proof, not the author's account. The divisibility of the theta kernel at each inert prime is the deepest local statement of the argument; we, like Rubinstein's guide, take it from the archive. For one variable on the formal group of one CM curve, a bound with this exponent is a theorem of Katz (Divisibilities, congruences, and Cartier duality, 1981), deduced from Lubin–Tate formal groups of height two; for the archive's version, in several variables over fields of growing degree, Jaswal's guide knows of no reference. Our checks at p = 5 are sanity checks of the congruence and the radius, not of that statement.
