Few bad members

The argument needs almost every member of its family to be well behaved. Here is how the proof knows that the bad ones are rare.

Why it matters

The proof does not work with one object but with a whole family of them. For the divisibility and the prohibition to combine, the family must be nonzero where it counts: a member whose local behaviour at some prime is degenerate contributes nothing useful, and if too many members were like that, the bound would collapse. So the proof needs to know that degenerate members are a small share, and how small.

Exact weights in a finite group

At each prime, the local behaviour of a member is described by a type, a representation of the finite group GLn(Fq) of invertible matrices over a field with q elements. J. A. Green worked out the characters of these groups in 1955. From his theory the proof reads off the exact weight of each local type, and the degenerate ones weigh about 1/q.

The flavour of this is easy to see in the smallest case. In GL2(Fq), the matrices whose two eigenvalues coincide are the degenerate ones, and their share is exactly

q / (q² − 1) ≈ 1/q

The tool below counts every invertible 2 × 2 matrix over Fq and checks this. It is an illustration of why degenerate things weigh about 1/q, not the archive's statement, which is about the types of representations in higher rank.

A small coincidence: q/(q² − 1) is the same expression as the weight of the gain at an inert prime on the Frobenius page. We have not looked for a link between the two and do not claim one.

The family follows the weights

Exact weights in a finite group would be of little use if the family ignored them. A counting theorem for automorphic forms, with an exponentially small error, shows that the members of the family are distributed according to these weights. Its only input from outside the archive is the Siegel–Weil formula for volumes.

The large gap between parameters that the archive asks for does its work here, in the counting theorem, and nowhere else that we found.
